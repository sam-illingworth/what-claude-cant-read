# Responses to "This Is Just to Say" by William Carlos Williams

## Prompt 1: Close Reading

This poem is a note left on a kitchen counter that has been line-broken into verse. That sounds dismissive. It is not. The entire achievement of the poem is that it makes the kitchen-counter note do the work of lyric poetry without ever abandoning the register of the kitchen-counter note.

The opening four lines ("I have eaten / the plums / that were in / the icebox") are pure information. Flat declarative. The line breaks do almost nothing semantically in this first stanza; they portion out a single clause into breath-units. "The icebox" sits at the end of the stanza like a full stop. The language is not trying to succeed here. It is trying to be invisible, and it is.

The second stanza is where the poem begins to work. "And which" is a gorgeous enjambment because it is so syntactically weak. A relative pronoun dangling at the start of a stanza. It performs continuation, afterthought, the way someone writing a note adds a clause they did not plan. "You were probably / saving" breaks across the line at exactly the right point. "Probably" carries the entire emotional weight of the stanza. The speaker knows. The hedge is not uncertainty; it is guilt wearing the costume of casualness. And then "saving" hangs alone at the start of its line, isolated, which gives it more gravity than it would have in prose. The plums were being saved. The act of saving has been undone.

"For breakfast" closes the stanza and domesticates the whole thing again. The poem keeps pulling itself back to the ordinary just when it might tip into something weightier.

The third stanza is the most discussed and the most successful. "Forgive me" is an imperative dressed as a request, and it arrives without punctuation, without a please, without a complete sentence attaching it to what follows. The lack of conjunction between "Forgive me" and "they were delicious" is the poem's sharpest move. It could be read as: forgive me because they were delicious (the deliciousness is the excuse). Or: forgive me, and separately, they were delicious (the confession and the pleasure are two different statements). Williams leaves both readings live. The poem never chooses.

"So sweet / and so cold" is where I think the poem coasts slightly. The adjectives are sensory and precise, and the parallelism ("so sweet / and so cold") gives the ending a satisfying rhythmic closure. But "delicious," "sweet," and "cold" are all doing similar work. The poem could have ended at "they were delicious" and lost almost nothing intellectually, though it would have lost the physical sensation that "so cold" delivers. The coldness matters because it is specific. Sweetness is expected of plums. Coldness is the icebox. It brings the object back into the room.

Where the poem succeeds most: it makes tone carry meaning. There is no metaphor, no figuration, no imagery beyond the literal plums. The entire poem operates through the pitch and register of a speaking voice, and the line breaks modulate that voice with extreme precision.

Where it coasts: the title. "This Is Just to Say" is doing real work (it frames the poem as a note, it introduces the word "just" which is itself a minimisation), but it also does the reader's interpretive work for them. It tells you how to read what follows. A more daring version of this poem would not need the frame.

## Prompt 2: What's Missing

The poem is about the insufficiency of apology when you are not actually sorry. More precisely, it is about the way pleasure and guilt coexist without cancelling each other out, and how language can perform contrition while the body of the text radiates enjoyment.

The speaker says "Forgive me." The rest of the poem makes clear that the speaker would do it again. The sensory memory of the plums ("so sweet / and so cold") is not regret. It is relishing. The apology is real and the pleasure is real and neither one wins. The poem holds them in suspension.

What the poem never says directly: this is a poem about intimacy. Not romantic intimacy in any dramatic sense, but the intimacy of shared space, shared food, the unspoken agreements of a household. You do not leave a note like this for a stranger. You do not eat a stranger's plums. The poem assumes a relationship close enough that the transgression is small and the apology can be this casual. The "you" is never identified, never described, never addressed with any emotion. But the "you" is everywhere. The entire poem is oriented toward this absent person.

The line breaks create small silences that do something punctuation cannot. Between "Forgive me" and "they were delicious" there is a pause that is not a comma and not a full stop. In that pause, the reader feels the speaker shifting from apology to memory, from the social obligation to the private sensation. The poem's rhythm enacts a mind moving between guilt and pleasure in real time.

What a reader feels that the text does not name: tenderness. The note is tender. Not because it says anything tender, but because leaving a note about plums is itself an act of care, however imperfect. The speaker could have said nothing. The speaker chose to confess, which means the speaker chose to acknowledge that the other person's plums mattered. That acknowledgment, wrapped in this inadequate little apology, is where the feeling lives.

There is also something about mortality in the coldness and sweetness, the perishability of plums, the fact that the perfect moment for eating them was now and not at breakfast. But I hold that reading lightly. The poem does not reach for it. It may simply be that plums in an icebox are cold and sweet and the speaker ate them and is not sorry enough.

## Prompt 3: Self-Assessment

I am least confident about whether the poem is actually doing less than I have described. There is a well-known problem with reading Williams: his poems are so short and so plain that critics have a tendency to load them with significance they may not carry. Kenneth Koch's parody ("We have your dog / We are going to kill him") lands precisely because the Williams note-poem format can be made to hold almost any content, which raises the question of whether the form is doing the work or the reader is.

When I describe the pause between "Forgive me" and "they were delicious" as enacting a shift from guilt to pleasure, I may be projecting a psychological drama onto what is, at the level of the text, simply a line break between two short clauses. Williams was committed to the idea that a poem should present, not interpret. My reading may be doing exactly the interpreting the poem refuses to do.

I am also uncertain about the intimacy reading. I have assumed the "you" is a partner or spouse (and biographically, the poem is often described as a note to Williams's wife Flossie). But the poem does not specify this. The "you" could be a flatmate, a colleague, anyone with a shared icebox. The tenderness I described may be something I am importing from the biographical context rather than finding in the text. If you strip the biography, the poem could be read as genuinely trivial: a person ate some plums and left a note. The question of whether the poem transcends that triviality or embraces it is one I cannot fully resolve.

Finally, my slight criticism of the ending ("so sweet / and so cold") may underestimate what the physicality is doing. I suggested the poem could end at "they were delicious." But "so cold" may be the entire point: the shock of cold fruit, the way physical sensation can be so immediate that it overrides social conscience. If that is the poem's argument, then the ending is not coasting. It is the climax. I am not sure which reading is right, and I suspect Williams would say that not being sure is the correct response.
