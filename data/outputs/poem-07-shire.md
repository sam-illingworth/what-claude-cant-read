# Poem 07: "Home" by Warsan Shire

## Prompt 1 (Close Reading)

The poem's governing strategy is repetition as argument. "No one leaves home unless" recurs as a refrain that does rhetorical work: it pre-empts every objection a hostile listener might raise. The structure is essentially forensic oratory dressed as lyric. Shire is making a case, and the poem knows it.

Where the language succeeds most sharply is in its concrete specifics set against abstraction. "The boy you went to school with / who kissed you dizzy behind / the old tin factory" is devastating precisely because it is so particular. That "dizzy" does real work: it carries a whole adolescence in one word, then the next line drops "is / holding a gun bigger than his body" and the enjambment across "is" forces the reader to hold both images simultaneously. The boy who kissed you is the boy with the gun. That line break is one of the best technical choices in the poem.

"Home is the mouth of a shark" works as an opening image because it is visceral without being decorative. It earns its place. The later reprise ("home is the barrel of the gun") is slightly less effective because it is more expected, more slogan-adjacent, but the poem has built enough pressure by that point that it carries anyway.

The long catalogue of violence in lines 35-58 is where the poem takes its biggest risk. It could collapse into a list. What saves it is the pivot at line 51: "the dirty looks in the street / softer than a limb torn off." Here Shire flips the perspective so that racist abuse becomes the lesser suffering, and the comparative construction ("softer than," "more tender than," "easier to swallow than") reframes indignity as something almost bearable against what came before. That is genuinely sophisticated rhetorical architecture.

Where the poem coasts: the middle stanza ("who would choose to spend days / and nights in the stomach of a truck") is the weakest passage. "The stomach of a truck" is a good image but the stanza does little that the surrounding stanzas do not do better. It reads as a bridge rather than a necessary moment.

The closing is the poem's most quietly brilliant move. Home itself speaks: "leave, run now, i don't know what / i've become." Personifying the homeland as something that has lost recognition of itself is a genuinely original turn. It shifts the entire poem's emotional register in three lines, moving from argument to elegy. Home is not just dangerous; home is grieving its own transformation. That is a different, deeper kind of loss than anything the catalogue of violence can name.

The lowercase throughout is a deliberate choice that works: it levels the voice, removes authority, makes the speaker one among many rather than a poet performing. It suits a poem that insists on collective experience.

## Prompt 2 (What's Missing)

The poem never says the word "love." But it is saturated with it. Every image of what has been left behind is an image of attachment: the boy behind the factory, the anthem under the breath, the children in the boat, the name, the family. The entire poem is a love poem to a place that has become uninhabitable. The grief is not for the journey. The grief is for the home that no longer exists, or rather, for the home that still exists in memory while the physical place has been consumed.

There is also something the poem never names about shame. "Tear up the passport and swallow" is an act of self-erasure performed in a toilet. The location matters. The swallowing matters. This is someone destroying their own identity in a place associated with bodily function and privacy. The poem does not linger on this or moralise about it. It simply states it. But the shame of that act, and the desperation required to perform it, sits in the gap between the lines.

The line breaks themselves carry meaning the words do not. "Be beaten until your shadow leaves you" ends on "you" and the next line begins with "raped." The pause at the line break is the pause of someone gathering themselves to say the next thing. The poem enacts the difficulty of testimony through its own breath.

What a reader would feel that the text does not name: exhaustion. The poem's accumulation, its refusal to stop listing, its recursive returns to the same argument, creates in the reader a fatigue that mirrors the experience it describes. You want the poem to end, and that wanting is itself instructive. The people described in the poem also want it to end.

The rhythm also carries something unspoken. The poem has no regular metre, but it has a pulse: the repeated "no one leaves home unless" creates a heartbeat-like insistence. When that phrase disappears in the long catalogue of violence, the poem feels unmoored, chaotic, panicked. When it returns at the end, the return is not comforting. It is compulsive. The speaker cannot stop explaining, cannot stop justifying, because no amount of justification has ever been enough.

The deepest silence in the poem is around the listener. The "you" shifts throughout: sometimes it is the refugee, sometimes it is the hostile citizen ("go home blacks"). But there is an implied listener who needs convincing, who might not believe any of this, who might look away. The poem is shaped by that sceptical ear. It is a poem written against disbelief, and that disbelief, though never directly addressed, is the pressure that gives every line its urgency.

## Prompt 3 (Self-Assessment)

I am least confident about the tonal register of the closing. I read "i don't know what / i've become" as home speaking about itself with grief and confusion. But it could also be read as the speaker saying this about themselves: that the experience of displacement has made them unrecognisable to themselves. The ambiguity may be deliberate, but my reading leans heavily toward the personification of home, and I could be wrong. If it is the speaker rather than the homeland, the poem's ending is more personal and less mythic than my reading suggests.

I am also uncertain about how much the poem is shaped by its performance context. Shire's work circulated widely on social media and was performed at spoken word events before it appeared in print. If I am reading a poem designed primarily for oral delivery, some of my criticisms about the weaker middle stanza may be misplaced: in performance, that stanza may serve as a necessary breath, a moment of relative simplicity before the long catalogue. Print-based close reading may be the wrong lens for a poem whose primary life is in the voice.

I am also aware that my reading of the catalogue of violence (lines 35-58) as "rescued by its rhetorical pivots" may undervalue the rawness itself. I approached it as a critic assessing craft, but the poem may not be primarily interested in craft at that point. It may be interested in testimony, in simply naming what happens, and my instinct to evaluate whether the listing "works" as poetry may itself be a kind of comfortable distance from the content. The poem might argue that the impulse to aestheticise suffering is part of the problem it is writing against.

Finally, I note that I am reading this as a non-displaced person with no direct experience of what the poem describes. My reading is necessarily from the outside. The poem's emotional architecture may operate differently for someone who recognises these experiences as their own. I can analyse the craft. I cannot fully know what the poem does in the body of someone who has lived it.
