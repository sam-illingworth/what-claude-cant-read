# Responses: "The Colonel" by Carolyn Forche

## Prompt 1: Close Reading

"The Colonel" operates through a sustained strategy of tonal flattening. The prose-poem form is doing most of the work here, and it is doing it brilliantly. By refusing lineation, Forche refuses the reader any pause, any white space in which to process. The domestic details and the atrocity arrive in the same syntactic register, the same unbroken block of text, and that equivalence is the poem's central engine.

The opening sentence is a masterstroke: "WHAT YOU HAVE HEARD is true." The capitalisation of the first clause performs the weight of testimony. It announces that this is not metaphor, not craft. It is deposited evidence. The sentence also pre-empts the reader's disbelief, which tells us something about the content before the content arrives.

The accumulation of domestic detail in the first half works through parataxis: short declarative sentences laid side by side without subordination. "His wife carried a tray of coffee and sugar. His daughter filed her nails, his son went out for the night." This is not description. It is inventory. The flatness is the point. The colonel's family life is rendered with the same syntactic weight as the pistol on the cushion, and that casual adjacency is more disturbing than any emphasis would be.

Where the poem genuinely excels is in the transitions it refuses to make. "Broken bottles were embedded in the walls around the house to scoop the kneecaps from a man's legs or cut his hands to lace" arrives without a conjunction, without a shift in tone. The brutality is stated with the same matter-of-factness as the rack of lamb. Forche trusts the reader to feel the whiplash. She does not perform it for us.

"The moon swung bare on its black cord over the house" is the single moment of overt figuration in the poem, and it earns its place precisely because it is alone. Against the documentary flatness, this one image (a moon like a bare bulb on a cord, suggestive of interrogation rooms, of poverty, of surveillance) carries enormous charge. Had there been three or four such images, the poem would have become literary in a way that would undercut its testimony. One image, placed early, establishes that this is a poet speaking, not a journalist, and then the poem proceeds to suppress that instinct entirely.

"They were like dried peach halves" is the other simile, and it functions differently. This is not poetic figuration. It is the mind reaching for the nearest domestic analogue to make the incomprehensible comprehensible. It is what you would actually think if you saw human ears on a table. The simile's banality is its horror.

"There is no other way to say this" is the poem's hinge. It is both an apology for the simile that preceded it (as if even "dried peach halves" aestheticises something that should not be aestheticised) and a defence of the entire poem's method. There is no other way to say this than flatly, without ornament, in prose.

The final two sentences are where Forche allows the poem to become, briefly, extraordinary. "Some of the ears on the floor caught this scrap of his voice. Some of the ears on the floor were pressed to the ground." The ears become listeners. They become the dead who are still hearing, still pressed to the earth of their own country. The repetition of "some of the ears on the floor" gives these sentences a liturgical quality, a dignity that the colonel has tried to strip from them. The poem ends not with the colonel's power but with the persistence of the mutilated. That is a stunning rhetorical move, and it costs Forche nothing in credibility because she has earned it through twenty lines of restraint.

Where does the poem coast? Almost nowhere. If I had to identify a moment of slight excess, it would be "My friend said to me with his eyes: say nothing." The phrase "with his eyes" is the kind of narrative shorthand that belongs more to prose fiction than to this poem's otherwise rigorous reportage. It is a small concession to dramatic convention. But it also does necessary work: it establishes the threat under which the speaker operates, so I would not cut it.

## Prompt 2: What's Missing

The poem never names the country (El Salvador), never names the colonel, never names the dead whose ears are on the table. It never says the word "torture." It never says "murder." It never says "war" or "dictatorship" or "United States foreign policy." It never tells us that the speaker is an American poet who has come to witness, or that the friend beside her is likely a local who lives under this regime every day.

What is present in the silence is complicity. The dinner is a performance staged for an American visitor. The rack of lamb, the good wine, the gold bell for the maid: this is a man showing the North American that he is civilised, that he is like her, that they share a class and a set of comforts. The poem never comments on this. It does not need to. The reader sits at that table too. We eat the lamb. We drink the wine. We are complicit in the scene before the ears arrive, and Forche makes us feel that complicity by making the domestic details seductive in their ordinariness.

The line breaks do not exist, and that absence is itself the meaning. A lineated poem would give us places to breathe, to step back, to assume the posture of aesthetic appreciation. The prose block denies this. You must read through. You cannot pause between the gold bell and the ears. The form enacts the experience of being in that room: there was no pause, no cut, no moment where the evening shifted from civilised to monstrous. It was always both.

The rhythm of the final sentences carries something the text does not name: grief. "Some of the ears on the floor caught this scrap of his voice. Some of the ears on the floor were pressed to the ground." The anaphora ("some of the ears on the floor") is a mourning rhythm. It is the closest the poem comes to elegy, and it arrives not for a named person but for anonymous body parts. What the reader feels is the weight of the uncountable dead, the people who have been reduced to evidence, to objects on a colonel's table. The poem refuses to restore their humanity through narrative (we never learn who they were), but it restores something through that final act of listening. The ears hear. The dead are not entirely dead. That is what the poem cannot say directly, because saying it directly would be sentimental, and sentimentality in the face of political murder is obscene.

There is also something the poem holds back about the speaker's own fear. "My friend said to me with his eyes: say nothing." The speaker is in danger. The colonel is showing her the ears as a threat, not just a boast. "Something for your poetry, no?" is a dare. He is telling her: write this, and see what happens. The poem is the answer to that dare, and its existence is an act of defiance that the text never frames as courage.

## Prompt 3: Self-Assessment

The element I am least confident about is whether the final image of the ears "pressed to the ground" is meant to evoke listening (as I read it) or subjugation, or both simultaneously. "Pressed to the ground" could mean pressed against the earth as if listening for vibrations (the dead still attentive to their own land), or it could mean pressed down, crushed, forced into the dirt. The difference matters: one reading grants the dead a kind of agency and persistence; the other emphasises their total defeat. I read the poem as doing both at once, with the listening reading slightly dominant because of the word "caught" in the preceding sentence (ears catching a voice implies the function of hearing). But I am aware that my reading may be shaped by a desire for the poem to end with resistance rather than with annihilation.

I am also uncertain about the parrot. "The parrot said hello on the terrace. The colonel told it to shut up." On one level this is documentary detail, the kind of thing that simply happened and so is reported. But the parrot is also a creature that repeats without understanding, and the colonel silencing it could be read as a figure for the regime's relationship to speech: repetition is tolerated, but only up to a point. I may be over-reading this. Forche's method in this poem is anti-symbolic; every detail earns its place by having actually occurred. Imposing symbolic weight on the parrot may be exactly the kind of literary reflex the poem is designed to resist.

Finally, I am reading this poem with knowledge of its context: Forche's time in El Salvador in 1978, the US-backed military government, the tens of thousands killed. A reader encountering this poem without that context would still feel its force, but they might read the colonel as an individual monster rather than as a representative of a system. My contextual knowledge may be making me read systemic critique where the poem is more interested in the irreducible specificity of one evening, one room, one sack of ears. The poem's power may lie precisely in its refusal to generalise, and my impulse to connect it to larger political structures may be a way of managing the discomfort of the particular.
