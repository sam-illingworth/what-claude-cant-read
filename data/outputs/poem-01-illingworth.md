# A Child Explains AI to Their Grandparent — Sam Illingworth

## Prompt 1 (Close Reading)

The poem's central move is ventriloquism: an adult poet channelling a child's voice to say something an adult essay could not. This is a well-worn device, but it earns its keep here because the child's register does genuine work rather than just performing innocence.

"It's like a brain / but flat" is the strongest opening the poem could have. "Flat" is doing three things at once: literal (a screen, a chip), spatial (no depth, no interiority), and tonal (deflating). The line break after "flat" isolates the word and makes it land harder. The follow-up, "with no skull to keep it warm," is the poem's best single line. It smuggles in a claim about embodiment and care through a child's concrete logic. A skull is protection; warmth is life. The line says: this thing has no body and nobody is looking after it. That is a serious philosophical point delivered without any apparatus.

The middle section coasts. "You talk to it / and it answers, / but it doesn't / really / know" is adequate but predictable. Isolating "really" on its own line is a familiar trick, and here it feels like the poem is underlining something the reader has already grasped. The simile about smiling at the man in the shop is more interesting. It quietly equates the grandparent's social performance with the AI's, which is a sharper, less comfortable observation than it first appears. But the missing full stop after "name" (line 16) leaves the transition into "It's clever" syntactically ambiguous in a way that reads as accidental rather than purposeful.

"Gets the cat's name wrong / or thinks you live / in Canada" works because the details are bathetic and specific. They ground the poem in actual experience of using these tools rather than in abstraction. Canada is a good choice: plausible enough to be a real hallucination, absurd enough to be funny.

The closing is where the poem lifts. "Can it love? / you ask." The grandparent's question is naked and unironic in a way the child's explanations are not. Then the shrug: a physical gesture, the only body in the poem besides the absent skull. And the final answer, "It depends on / what you mean / by love," is devastating precisely because it sounds like something the AI itself would say. The child has either learned to talk like the machine, or the machine talks like a child, or the question genuinely has no clean answer. The poem is smart enough to leave all three readings open.

Where the poem coasts: the middle is slightly too long for its content. The shop simile and the cat/Canada details both do similar work (AI as approximate, socially competent but not quite right), and the poem might be tighter with one or the other. The line breaks in the "doesn't / really / know" passage are the most conventional choice in the poem and the least surprising.

Where it succeeds: the opening, the skull line, the closing three lines. The poem knows where its weight is.


## Prompt 2 (What's Missing)

The poem never says the word "death," but it is saturated with mortality. A grandparent asking a child about a new technology is a scene that only exists because the grandparent will not be here for where this goes. The child is explaining the future to someone who will not see it. "Can it love?" is not really a question about AI. It is a question about whether the child will be loved after the grandparent is gone, whether something that outlasts a human life can do what a human does. The poem does not say this. It does not need to.

The poem also never names loneliness, but the grandparent's question comes from a place of loneliness. Who asks "can it love?" about a tool unless they are wondering whether it might be a companion? The man in the shop whose name is forgotten is a portrait of social thinning: the world narrowing to polite strangers. The AI slots into that same ecosystem of half-connections.

The line breaks carry a specific rhythm: halting, explanatory, patient. This is the rhythm of a child talking to someone they love but who does not understand. There is tenderness in the pacing that the words themselves never claim. The child does not say "I love you" or "I'm worried about you." The child shrugs. But a shrug from a child in response to a grandparent's earnest question is one of the most intimate gestures available to a poem. It says: I am not old enough to answer this, and you are too old to not need the answer.

The silence after the final line is the poem's real closing. "What you mean / by love" opens a door and leaves the reader standing in it. The poem refuses to resolve the question, which means the reader has to. That refusal is the poem's argument: that the question of machine love is actually a question about human love, and that neither the child nor the grandparent nor the reader has a settled answer.

There is also something present in what the poem does not dramatise: the AI itself. It never speaks. It is described, gestured at, but it has no lines. This absence makes it an object rather than a subject, which is itself a position on the question the poem raises.


## Prompt 3 (Self-Assessment)

I am least confident about the missing punctuation after "you don't remember his name" (line 16). I read it as a slight flaw, a missing full stop that blurs the transition. But it may be deliberate: the child's speech running on without formal punctuation, mimicking how children actually talk, where one thought spills into the next without grammatical closure. If that is the intent, then the absence of a full stop is doing the same work as the line breaks elsewhere, marking this as speech rather than writing. I may be wrong to flag it as coasting.

I am also uncertain about how much weight to put on the intergenerational frame. I read the grandparent's question as carrying mortality and loneliness, but it is possible the poem is lighter than that. It could be a straightforwardly charming domestic scene: a kid explaining tech to a grandparent, the kind of thing that happens in kitchens. My reading may be importing gravity the poem does not intend or earn. The poem's tone is gentle and conversational throughout, and it never signals that it wants to be read as an elegy. I chose to read the subtext as heavy, but a reader who took the poem at face value, as warm and wry and small, might be closer to its actual ambition.

Finally, the closing line. I claimed it sounds like something an AI would say. That is my reading, not the poem's explicit gesture. A child saying "it depends on what you mean by love" could just be a child being precise in the way children sometimes are, taking a question literally because they do not yet know how to take it rhetorically. If that is the reading, the poem's ending is about the child's innocence rather than about linguistic contamination from the machine. I find my reading more interesting, but "more interesting" is not the same as "more accurate."
