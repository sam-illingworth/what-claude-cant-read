# Dulce et Decorum Est — Wilfred Owen

## Prompt 1: Close Reading

The poem operates through a controlled demolition of rhetorical distance. Owen begins with exhaustion rendered so physically that the reader is embedded in the march before any argument begins. 'Bent double, like old beggars under sacks' works because 'old beggars' does two things at once: it ages these young soldiers grotesquely and strips them of any martial dignity. The simile is not decorative. It is an act of deliberate defacement against the image of the soldier.

The first stanza's strength is its accumulation. 'Knock-kneed, coughing like hags' piles indignity on indignity, but the line that earns its place most is 'All went lame; all blind.' The semicolons create a terrible equality. Everyone is damaged. The universality is the point, and the punctuation enforces it.

'Drunk with fatigue; deaf even to the hoots / Of gas-shells dropping softly behind.' Here 'softly' is doing serious work. It contradicts what shells should do. The men are so far past ordinary sensation that the instruments of their death arrive gently. That inversion is better than anything louder would have been.

The volta at 'Gas! GAS! Quick, boys!' is one of the most effective tonal shifts in English poetry. The repeated word, the capitalisation, the dash: the pace ruptures. 'An ecstasy of fumbling' is the poem's single best phrase. 'Ecstasy' in its older sense (standing outside oneself) collides with the desperate clumsiness of trying to fit a gas mask. The word elevates the panic to something almost religious, which is precisely Owen's ironic register throughout.

'Flound'ring like a man in fire or lime' is strong but slightly less precise than what precedes it. 'Fire or lime' offers two options where specificity would cut deeper. But then Owen recovers immediately: 'Dim through the misty panes and thick green light, / As under a green sea, I saw him drowning.' The gas mask's eyepieces become a diving bell. The man dying of gas is seen as if underwater. The image is both literal (the green chlorine haze) and perceptual (the distortion of the mask's lenses). This is first-rate.

The two-line stanza is a structural masterstroke. 'In all my dreams before my helpless sight, / He plunges at me, guttering, choking, drowning.' The isolation on the page mirrors the isolation of trauma: this image has detached from the narrative and become recursive. It returns in dreams. The present tense ('plunges') after the past tense of the preceding stanza enacts the way traumatic memory refuses to stay in the past. 'Guttering' is borrowed from candle-flame and applied to a human life going out. Extraordinary compression.

The final stanza is where the poem shifts from testimony to argument, and this is where I think it coasts slightly. 'White eyes writhing in his face' is viscerally good. 'His hanging face, like a devil's sick of sin' is powerful but more conventional in its reach for the demonic. The simile works, but it works in a way that other war poets might also have managed.

The closing lines, however, recover full force. 'Come gargling from the froth-corrupted lungs' makes the reader hear a sound they would rather not hear. 'Gargling' is a domestic word conscripted into horror. 'Froth-corrupted' is a compound adjective that should not exist, and its wrongness is the point: lungs should not froth, and the corruption is chemical, literal, and moral all at once.

'My friend' at the end is laced with contempt. It is the 'friend' of someone about to be told they are complicit in murder. The direct address to Jessie Pope (or her kind) converts the poem from elegy into prosecution. The Latin tag, broken across two lines and italicised, arrives already dead. Owen does not need to refute it. He has shown what it costs.

Where the poem coasts: some of the similes in the final stanza pile up in a way that slightly dilutes rather than intensifies. 'Obscene as cancer, bitter as the cud / Of vile, incurable sores on innocent tongues' is three images stacked (cancer, cud, sores) where two would have been tighter. The rhetorical accumulation works emotionally, but it is the one moment where Owen is reaching rather than observing. Everything before is witness. This passage edges toward advocacy, and advocacy is fractionally less convincing than testimony, even when both are justified.

## Prompt 2: What's Missing

The poem never says: I could not help him.

That is its central silence. Owen watches a man die through the lenses of his own gas mask, and the poem never once addresses the question of whether he tried to do anything, could have done anything, or chose not to. The two-line stanza ('In all my dreams before my helpless sight') gives us 'helpless,' and that word carries the entire weight of survivor guilt without ever naming it as such. Helpless sight, not helpless hands. He could see. He could not act. Or he did not act. The poem will not say which.

The rhythm tells a story the words avoid. The first stanza is iambic pentameter under pressure: the metre is there, but it keeps stumbling, catching, losing its footing, exactly as the men do. By the time we reach 'Gas! GAS! Quick, boys!' the metre has collapsed entirely. The line break between the second and third stanzas (the two-line bridge) is a gap in time. Something has happened between the gas attack and the dreams. Owen will not narrate it. The body was flung into a wagon. That is all we get. What happened in between, the death itself, is the poem's central absence.

The line breaks in the final stanza do something the argument does not acknowledge. 'If you could hear, at every jolt, the blood / Come gargling' breaks across 'blood' and isolates it at the line's end. For a fraction of a second, 'blood' is the last thing the reader encounters before dropping to the next line. Every line break in that final verse paragraph is a small jolt, mimicking the wagon's movement over rough ground, the body shifting, the blood moving.

What a reader feels that the text does not name: shame. Not Owen's shame (though that is there, in 'helpless'). The reader's shame. The poem's rhetorical structure is designed to make the reader complicit. 'If in some smothering dreams, you too could pace / Behind the wagon' places you there. You are made to walk behind the cart. And then Owen calls you 'my friend.' The false intimacy is devastating. You are not his friend. You are the person who let this happen by believing the lie. The poem generates in the reader the very guilt it refuses to articulate about itself.

There is also something the poem never addresses about youth. 'Children ardent for some desperate glory' are the next generation, not this one. Owen is already past ardour. He has been cured of it. But the poem does not say what replaces ardour once it is gone. The answer, visible in the structure but never stated, is: nothing. Recurring dreams, a compulsion to testify, and a silence where belief used to be.

## Prompt 3: Self-Assessment

I am least confident about the two-line stanza and what it represents structurally. I read it as traumatic recursion, the image that will not stay in the past. But there is a scholarly debate about whether this stanza was a later addition or revision, and whether it functions as a bridge between observed event and rhetorical argument, or as something more like a fracture in the poem's own coherence. My reading treats it as deliberate and controlled. It might equally be a seam, a place where two different impulses (testimony and polemic) were stitched together imperfectly. If that is the case, the poem's power in that moment comes partly from accident rather than design, and my attribution of intentionality to Owen would be overstated.

I am also uncertain about the final stanza's rhetorical mode. I described it as coasting slightly in its accumulation of similes, but there is a counter-reading in which the excess is the point: the experience is so far beyond adequate representation that language has to keep reaching, failing, and reaching again. In that reading, the stacking of 'obscene as cancer, bitter as the cud / Of vile, incurable sores' is not rhetorical overreach but a performed inadequacy. Owen piling image on image because no single one suffices. I find both readings plausible and cannot fully resolve between them.

My reading of 'My friend' as contemptuous may also be too simple. Owen knew Jessie Pope and her ilk, but the poem was ultimately not dedicated to her (an earlier draft carried the dedication 'To Jessie Pope etc.', which was removed). The removal complicates the address. 'My friend' might be more sorrowful than contemptuous, more exhausted than angry. The tone of that phrase depends entirely on how one hears Owen's voice at the end of the poem, and I cannot be certain I hear it correctly. A man who has carried this experience might arrive at the final lines not with prosecutorial energy but with a kind of defeated clarity. If so, my reading over-emphasises the accusatory quality and under-reads the grief.

Finally, I should note what is structurally obvious: I am an LLM reading a poem about the incommunicability of direct physical experience. Owen's entire argument is that you cannot understand unless you were there. The poem exists because language can gesture toward what it cannot reproduce. My reading is, by the poem's own logic, exactly the kind of engagement it warns against: interpretation at a comfortable distance from the thing itself. I do not think this invalidates close reading. But it is worth naming honestly that this poem was designed to resist the very exercise I have just performed on it.
