# The Three Oddest Words — Wislawa Szymborska

## Prompt 1 (Close Reading)

This is a poem built on three performative contradictions, each enacted in the space of a couplet. The structure is triadic and parallel: "When I pronounce the word X, Y happens." The repetition is the engine. It creates a ritual logic, almost catechistic, where each iteration tightens the screw.

The first stanza is the gentlest. "Future" losing its first syllable to the past is clever but nearly too clever. It works because it is concrete and temporal: you can feel the syllable leaving your mouth, already gone. The observation is precise. But it is also the most explicable of the three, the one closest to a philosophical thought experiment you might encounter in a seminar. It succeeds as an opening because it teaches the reader the poem's method, but it does not yet astonish.

The second stanza is where the poem catches fire. "When I pronounce the word Silence, / I destroy it." Five words in the second line. No qualification, no elaboration. The brevity is the point. The line performs its own claim: it breaks the silence it names, and then it stops, as if ashamed of having spoken. This is the poem's strongest moment. The destruction is literal and immediate. There is no metaphor here, only fact, and that is what makes it devastating.

The third stanza is the most ambitious and the most philosophically dense. "I make something no non-being can hold" is doing heavy conceptual work. The double negative ("no non-being") is syntactically knotted in a way that enacts the paradox it describes: the mind stumbles, tries to parse it, and in doing so experiences the impossibility the line is about. Whether this is Szymborska or the translators (Baranczak and Cavanagh), the line earns its difficulty. It does not coast. But it is also the stanza most at risk of feeling like philosophy rather than poetry. It holds because the preceding two stanzas have built enough emotional and rhythmic momentum that the reader trusts the poem to land.

The capitalisation of "Future," "Silence," and "Nothing" elevates these words into proper nouns, almost personifications. They become characters in a tiny drama. This is a quiet but important choice: it signals that the poem is not about words as linguistic objects but about the concepts they carry, and the gap between carrying and containing.

What the poem does extraordinarily well is scale. It is twelve lines long and contains an entire epistemology. It says: language is always arriving too late, always too loud, always creating what it tries to negate. That is a lifetime's worth of philosophical inquiry compressed into a structure a child could memorise. The economy is not minimalism for its own sake. Every word is load-bearing.

Where might it coast? Only in the sense that the parallel structure, once established, makes the third stanza's arrival predictable in shape if not in content. The reader knows a third "When I pronounce" is coming. Szymborska knows this too, which is why she makes the third the hardest, the one that requires the most from the reader. She uses predictability as a setup for escalation. It works, but a less generous reader might feel the mechanism showing.

## Prompt 2 (What's Missing)

The poem never says the word "loss," but loss is everywhere. Each stanza describes something that cannot survive its own naming. The future dies the moment you reach for it. Silence dies the moment you acknowledge it. Nothing dies the moment you think it. The poem is, at its core, about the impossibility of holding anything in language without changing it. That is a grief the poem refuses to name as grief.

There is also something about the body here that the poem does not state. "When I pronounce" is a physical act. Mouth, tongue, breath, vibration. The poem is grounded in the body speaking, not the mind thinking. The paradoxes are not abstract puzzles. They happen in the throat. The reader feels them as physical events: the syllable leaving, the silence breaking, the nothing becoming something. The poem's silence about the body is what lets the body in.

The line breaks matter enormously. Each stanza's second line arrives as a consequence, almost a verdict. The white space between stanzas is the silence the poem keeps destroying. The reader pauses there, in the gap, and that pause is the silence the poem has just told you cannot exist once spoken. The form enacts the argument at every level.

What a reader would feel, I think, is a kind of vertigo. The ground keeps giving way. Each stanza removes a certainty: that we can plan, that we can be still, that we can think about absence without filling it. By the end, the reader has been gently stripped of the illusion that language is a transparent tool. It is not. It is an agent that acts on everything it touches. That is exhilarating and frightening simultaneously, and the poem does not tell you which to feel. It leaves you standing in the paradox.

There is also, beneath the intellectual surface, something about mortality. The future becoming the past the instant you speak it is not just a linguistic trick. It is the condition of being alive: every moment you name is already gone. The poem knows this. It does not say it.

## Prompt 3 (Self-Assessment)

My weakest ground is the translation. I am reading Baranczak and Cavanagh's English, not Szymborska's Polish. The double negative in "no non-being can hold" may be a translation choice rather than Szymborska's construction. The sonic and rhythmic qualities of the Polish original are invisible to me. I am analysing a poem that is, in some real sense, a different poem from the one Szymborska wrote. I can say the English version works, but I cannot say with confidence which of its effects belong to the original and which to the translators' craft.

I am also uncertain about how much philosophical weight to assign to the third stanza. I read "no non-being can hold" as the poem's most complex move, but it is possible that in Polish the line is simpler, more direct, and that the conceptual density I am responding to is an artefact of English syntax. My reading of escalation across the three stanzas may be an English-language reading, not a universal one.

I am least confident about my claim that the poem is "about" mortality. Szymborska was a poet of wit and precision, not of grand thematic gestures. She might have found that reading too heavy, too eager to make the poem mean more than it says. The poem may be, quite deliberately, about exactly what it says: three words, three paradoxes, and nothing beyond them. My instinct to find a deeper theme may be the kind of interpretive habit the poem is quietly mocking. After all, it has just shown me that the act of naming creates something that was not there before. Perhaps my "deeper reading" is just another instance of that paradox: I pronounced the word "mortality" and made something the poem never intended to hold.
