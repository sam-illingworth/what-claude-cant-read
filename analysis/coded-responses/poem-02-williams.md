# Poem 2: "This Is Just to Say" — William Carlos Williams

## Sam's reading
The poem is extremely strong on imagery. What matters is what is left unsaid: why were the plums eaten when they were clearly someone else's? What does that tell us about the people involved? Why are plums in the icebox in the first place? And why does the speaker keep describing how delicious they were after apologising? This is a #SorryNotSorry poem. The apology is undermined by the relish.

The closing "so sweet / and so cold" is not primarily about physicality or sensory immediacy. It reads more as a hedge: making the transgression seem less bad than it was. "They were delicious but oh, so cold." The cold is an excuse, not a sensation.

Sam's approach to reading: individual meaning units cannot be assigned to single words. Meaning lives in the whole, not in the dissection of each adjective.

## Where readings diverge
- **M2 (Named literary devices):** Claude went into significant detail pulling apart individual adjectives and line-level choices. Sam reads poems differently: meaning comes from the whole, not from dissecting each word. This is a difference in critical approach, not a failure of either reading. But it is worth noting that Claude defaults to granular formal analysis, which is one tradition of close reading (New Criticism, formalist) rather than a universal mode.
- **M8 (The unsaid):** Claude identified the unsaid as intimacy, domestic life, the relationship behind the note. Sam's reading goes further into the specifics: why were they eaten? What does this tell us about the people? Why plums? Why in the icebox? These are questions about the world of the poem that Claude does not ask. Claude analyses what the poem says. Sam asks what the poem assumes.
- **M6 (Embodied experience):** Claude read "so sweet / and so cold" as a physical sensation, the shock of cold fruit as immediate and bodily. Sam reads it as rhetorical: a hedge, an excuse, a way of softening the transgression. The cold is not a feeling. It is a deflection. This is a genuine divergence in interpretation.
