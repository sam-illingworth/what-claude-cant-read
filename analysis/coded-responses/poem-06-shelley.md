# Poem 6: "Ozymandias" — Percy Bysshe Shelley

## Sam's reading
The poem is about transience. You can be powerful one day and nothing the next. Power never lasts forever. It is a very beautiful poem: extremely structured, nothing wasted, and despite its age it still feels real. That freshness may come from the distance between the poet and the imaginary subject matter.

A five-year-old who had never encountered this poem before would produce a less polished reading than Claude's. It would also be much more interesting.

## What Claude detected well
- **M1 (Surface structure):** Yes. Nested narration, the sonnet form, the rhythmic drop at "Nothing beside remains."
- **M2 (Named literary devices):** Yes. Detailed as usual.
- **M3 (Thematic summary):** Good. Impermanence, irony, the gap between the inscription and the emptiness.
- **M8 (The unsaid):** Good. Identified the unnamed death, the absent works, the loneliness.
- **M11 (Self-awareness):** Flagged uncertainty about "the heart that fed," the rhyme scheme, and whether the poem offers consolation or refuses it. Also raised the possibility that Shelley is writing about the sculptor (and by extension the poet) as the hidden hero. Reasonable.

## Where readings diverge
- **M3 (Thematic summary):** Claude called the poem "genuinely brilliant." Sam's question: is it genuinely brilliant, or is Claude predicting that it is brilliant because a thousand essays say so? This is the core problem with well-studied poems. LLMs predict the most likely next token. If the training data contains overwhelmingly positive critical commentary on Ozymandias, Claude will produce a positive reading. It cannot do otherwise. This is not analysis. It is consensus reproduction.
- **M8 (The unsaid):** Claude said the poem never says the word "death." It does not say "death," but it does contain "loss" implicitly throughout. Sam's reading is simpler and more direct: the poem is about transience and the impermanence of power. Claude's reading adds layers (loneliness, the sculptor as artist-hero, art outlasting empire) that may be valid but may also be inherited from the critical tradition rather than derived from the text.

## Pattern: consensus as analysis
This poem is the clearest test case for the "projected reader" pattern identified in Poem 5. Ozymandias has been studied, anthologised, and essayed more than almost any English-language sonnet. Claude's reading is competent, polished, and indistinguishable from a thousand other close readings. It contains no original observation. Everything it says has been said before, because everything it knows about this poem comes from what has been said before.

A human encountering this poem for the first time could produce a genuinely novel reading. They could misread it, be confused by it, connect it to something personal, find it boring, find it life-changing. Claude cannot do any of these things. It can only produce the weighted average of all prior readings. For a well-studied poem, that average is fluent and correct. It is also, by definition, unoriginal.

Sam's framing: a five-year-old's reading would be less valid by academic standards. It would be far more interesting. The study's question is whether "interesting" matters more than "correct" when the goal is comprehension rather than criticism.
