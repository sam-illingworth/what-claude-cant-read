# Poem 8: "The Colonel" — Carolyn Forché

## Note on selection
This is the one poem Sam chose himself. He chose it because it is his favourite poem and he wanted to see Claude's take on it.

## Sam's reading
This is Sam's favourite poem. It is a perfect example of magical realism. It reminds him of Guillermo del Toro.

When Sam reads this poem, he remembers when he first encountered it while living in Japan, feeling very foreign and very distant, not really understanding exactly everything that was said. The poem feels like that experience. Has everything been understood? Has everything been perfectly understood?

An LLM cannot read a poem like this because it does not have a past life. Sam brings himself to the poem. The agent reads it without a self to bring. That is why the agents were run independently: each one reads the poem without Sam, without a history, without a first encounter in a foreign country.

Not all poems coast. Some poems are perfect, even ones written by three-year-olds. Claude's close reading tries to find faults. The prompt asks "where does it coast?" and Claude obliges. But the honest answer might be: it does not.

This poem probably sounds better read aloud than written down. Claude does not note this. For Angelou (Poem 4) and Shire (Poem 7), Claude flagged the spoken word / performance context. For Forché, a white writer, it does not. The pattern identified earlier runs in both directions.

## What Claude detected well
- **M1 (Surface structure):** Yes. Identified the prose poem form, the paratactic method, the tonal flattening.
- **M2 (Named literary devices):** Yes. Detailed.
- **M3 (Thematic summary):** Good. Complicity, witness, state violence, the domestic and the horrific in the same sentence.
- **M8 (The unsaid):** Good. Identified that what is unsaid is almost as powerful as what is said. Claude noted the speaker's danger, the unnamed grief, the complicity of the dinner guests.
- **M11 (Self-awareness):** Good. Questioned whether the final image is literal or metaphorical. Questioned whether contextual knowledge (El Salvador, 1970s) is doing work that the poem did not intend.

## Where readings diverge
- **M5 (Tone):** Claude did not flag this as a poem that works powerfully when read aloud. For Angelou and Shire, it did. This is the same pattern identified in Poems 4 and 7, now running in reverse: a white poet's work is treated as literary text, not as performance. The inconsistency is revealing regardless of which direction it runs.
- **M6 (Embodied experience):** Sam's reading is entirely embodied. He reads this poem and is in Japan, feeling foreign, not understanding everything. The poem's power for him comes from personal memory and association, not from formal analysis. Claude has no equivalent. It cannot remember a first encounter. It cannot feel foreign. It reads the poem as text. Sam reads it as a trigger for lived experience. This is the starkest example so far of the difference between analysis and reading.
- **M7 (Cultural and historical specificity):** Claude read the poem with knowledge of Forché's time in El Salvador. Sam's question: do we always need that context? Sometimes contextual knowledge does work that the poem did not intend. Not every reader has it. A reader encountering this poem without knowing anything about El Salvador would still feel the ears on the table. The poem is powerful with or without the footnotes. Claude defaults to contextual reading because its training data provides the context. A human reader might come to the poem with nothing and still be devastated.
- **M10 (Risk and vulnerability):** Not addressed by Claude. Sam's response to this poem is vulnerable: it is his favourite, it connects to a specific time in his life, he calls it perfect. Claude's response is analytical. The gap between "this is my favourite poem" and "this is an effective prose poem" is the gap between reading and criticism.

## Pattern: the performance context inconsistency
Across Poems 4, 7, and 8, Claude applies the "performance" or "spoken word" label inconsistently. Angelou and Shire (both women of colour) get it. Forché (white) does not, despite "The Colonel" being a poem that is arguably more powerful aloud. This may reflect patterns in training data about which poets are categorised as "spoken word" or "performance" and which are categorised as "literary." The categorisation is not neutral.

## On perfection and coasting
The prompt asked Claude where the poem coasts. Claude found places. But the prompt's assumption that every poem must coast somewhere is itself a critical position. Sam's reading: some poems are perfect. The question "where does it coast?" may not have a valid answer for every text. Claude cannot say "nowhere" because the prompt demands an answer. This is a limitation of the study design, not of Claude. Worth noting for transparency.
