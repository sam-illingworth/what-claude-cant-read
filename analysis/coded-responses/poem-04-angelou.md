# Poem 4: "Still I Rise" — Maya Angelou

## Sam's reading
The poem is about a repressed woman having her voice heard and forcing it to be heard. It uses words that have been used against the speaker and claims them back. It owns blackness. The poem is rooted in anger and pain but is ultimately hopeful.

This is about structural racism, not individual racial insults. Slavery is not directly mentioned until the end, but what the poem is about is obvious throughout.

## Sam's positionality
Sam is a white, heterosexual, cis-gendered, middle-class man. This should be stated in the post. Both the poet's reading and the model's reading are shaped by positionality. Neither is neutral.

## What Claude detected well
- **M1 (Surface structure):** Yes. Identified the structural escalation from quatrains to incantatory free verse.
- **M2 (Named literary devices):** Yes. Detailed. Identified the economic similes (oil wells, gold mines, diamonds) as reclaiming extracted wealth.
- **M3 (Thematic summary):** Good. Defiance, reclamation, the address to an oppressor.
- **M8 (The unsaid):** Good. Identified grief underneath defiance, the exhaustion of having to keep rising, the history of sexual violence behind "diamonds at the meeting of my thighs."
- **M11 (Self-awareness):** Acknowledged that a purely formal reading cannot account for what the poem does in the world as a cultural object.

## Where readings diverge
- **M5 (Tone):** Claude described the poem as "built for the voice" and characterised it as a performance poem. Sam questions this framing. Why is this poem "built for the voice" when Wilfred Owen's is not? Owen's poem is equally powerful when read aloud, equally rooted in direct address, equally structured around escalation. The distinction may reveal an assumption: that Black poetry, or poetry of protest, is oral and performative, while canonical war poetry is literary and textual. Claude may not intend this, but the framing carries it. This is worth examining: does Claude treat different traditions with different critical vocabularies, and if so, what does that reflect about training data?
- **M7 (Cultural and historical specificity):** Claude acknowledged the cultural context is "not purely literary." But it made this acknowledgement specifically for Angelou and not, for instance, for Owen. Both poems are deeply embedded in historical specificity. Both require cultural knowledge to read fully. The selective application of "cultural context" as a caveat is itself a pattern worth noting. It suggests Claude treats some poems as universal literature and others as culturally situated, which is not a neutral distinction.
- **M8 (The unsaid):** Claude identified that slavery is not mentioned until the final stanza. Sam's reading is that it is obvious throughout what this poem is about. The unsaid is not hidden. It is the foundation. Claude treats the absence of the word "slavery" as a literary technique (delayed revelation). Sam reads it as unnecessary because the poem's meaning is self-evident to anyone with the relevant knowledge.

## Pattern emerging
Claude applies different critical frameworks to different poems in ways that may correlate with the poet's identity. Angelou gets "performance poem" and "cultural context." Owen gets formal analysis and revision history. Both are poems of witness. Both are rooted in embodied, historical experience. The question is why Claude reaches for different vocabularies when reading them.
