# Poem 10: "The Three Oddest Words" — Wisława Szymborska (trans. Barańczak & Cavanagh)

## Note on selection
This poem was selected by Claude to fill the non-English tradition slot. Sam had not encountered it before this study. This is the only poem in the set that Sam read for the first time during the coding process.

## Sam's reading
Sam loves this poem. It feels like a riddle, something from Tolkien. Reading it triggered a memory of reading The Hobbit at ten years old, sitting in primary school at Grove Road School, and the riddles in that book. This is what poems do: they open doors into a reader's past that the poet could not have predicted and the text does not contain.

The line "I make something no non-being can hold" feels pointed towards large language models, even though the poem predates them. It was interesting that Claude selected this poem. Perhaps it recognised something in it.

The poem is a translation. When reading a translation, you are analysing a poem that is different from the one originally written. What happens when the original Polish is read? The performative contradictions (saying "silence" breaks it, saying "nothing" creates something) may work differently in Polish. The translation is a second poem, not a copy of the first.

## What Claude detected well
- **M1 (Surface structure):** Yes. Identified the triadic structure, the performative contradictions.
- **M2 (Named literary devices):** Yes. Paradox, self-referentiality.
- **M3 (Thematic summary):** Good. Language as both tool and obstacle. The impossibility of naming without changing.
- **M8 (The unsaid):** This is the one poem where Claude's reading of the unsaid actually made Sam think something different. Claude identified loss as everywhere: "Silence dies the moment you acknowledge it." Sam had not read the poem that way before. This is notable because it is the first time in the study that Claude's interpretation genuinely shifted the human reader's understanding, rather than restating what the human already felt.
- **M11 (Self-awareness):** Good. Identified the translation as its weakest ground.

## Where readings diverge
- **M2 (Named literary devices):** Claude again looked for where the poem coasts. Sam's view: it does not coast. It is six lines long. There is nothing to cut. The prompt's assumption that every poem has a weakness is again a limitation of the study design (as noted in Poem 8).
- **M6 (Embodied experience):** Sam reads this poem and is ten years old in primary school reading The Hobbit. Claude has no equivalent. It cannot know that Sam was at Grove Road School, or that the riddle-like quality of Szymborska's paradoxes would trigger Tolkien. This is the most direct illustration in the study of what a human brings to a poem that a model cannot: a lifetime of associations, memories, and connections that are invisible to the text and invisible to anyone who is not that specific reader.
- **M7 (Cultural and historical specificity):** Claude acknowledged the translation issue but did not go further. Sam's point is that the poem in English is a different poem from the one in Polish. The performative contradictions depend on how the words sound, how the syllables fall, what connotations the Polish words carry. Claude is reading a translation and treating it as the poem. Every translation is an interpretation. Claude is reading an interpretation of an interpretation.
- **M10 (Risk and vulnerability):** Claude confidently described Szymborska as "a poet of wit and precision, not of grand thematic gestures." Sam's question: how do you know that? Even if existing criticism supports this characterisation, how does Claude know the poet did not want to write something different? Claude pins poets into boxes based on their critical reception. It cannot access their intentions, their ambitions, or the gap between what they achieved and what they wanted to achieve. This is not unique to LLMs. Critics do it too. But Claude does it with particular confidence because it has no uncertainty about the characterisation. It is simply reproducing the consensus.

## Key finding: the one moment Claude added something
This is the only poem in the study where Claude's reading genuinely introduced a new perspective for Sam. The mortality reading ("loss is everywhere, silence dies the moment you acknowledge it") was not something Sam had considered. This may be because Sam had no prior relationship with the poem. For every other poem, Sam came with existing associations, memories, and interpretations. For this one, Claude's reading arrived first.

This suggests something important: Claude's readings may be most valuable when the human reader has the least prior relationship with the text. When the reader already knows a poem, Claude reproduces what they have already read or felt. When the reader is encountering something new, Claude can function as a first interlocutor, offering a reading that opens rather than closes the text. The value of Claude as a reader may be inversely proportional to the reader's existing familiarity.

## Pattern: pinning poets into boxes
Claude characterises poets with confidence based on critical consensus. Szymborska is "wit and precision." Angelou is "performance." Frost is "deceptively simple." These are received characterisations, not original readings. They may be accurate, but they foreclose the possibility that a poet might have wanted to be something other than what they became known for. Claude reads poets as their reputations. Humans can read poets as people.
